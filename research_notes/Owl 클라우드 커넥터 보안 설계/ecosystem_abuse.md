# Third-party API/OAuth ecosystem abuse of personal data: incidents and platform controls that worked

Scope: OAuth/API ecosystem abuse and platform governance only. Prompt injection and MCP tool poisoning are out of scope because earlier research covered them. Research date: 2026-11-08.

Verification legend:
- **[V]**: verified in this session against a primary source (vendor advisory, FTC, GTIG, peer-reviewed paper) or against consistent reporting from several outlets.
- **[S]**: secondary reporting only, from security vendors or trade press.
- **[T]**: well known, but this session did not re-fetch the source. The URL is the canonical page as I recall it, so the report writer should spot-check it.

## 1. Consent phishing and malicious or compromised OAuth apps

### Takeaway
Attackers rarely break the OAuth protocol. Instead they either (a) get a user to grant a token to an attacker-controlled app, using fake names and branding, borrowed "verified" badges, or a phone call that walks the victim through the grant, or (b) steal the long-lived refresh tokens that a legitimate integrator stores, which makes every customer of that integrator a victim at once. The 2025–2026 wave (Salesloft Drift → Gainsight → Context.ai/Vercel) shows the second pattern is now dominant. The platform-side responses that actually contained these incidents were mass revocation of all tokens for one client ID, removing the app from the marketplace, and publishing the client ID as an indicator of compromise (IOC).

### Cited Findings

**2017 Google Docs OAuth worm (2017-05-03) [V]**
- Phishing emails posing as Google Docs led recipients to authorize a malicious OAuth app named "Google Docs." The app then mailed the same lure to each victim's contacts, so it spread like a worm. — [The Next Web, 2017-05-03](https://thenextweb.com/news/massive-google-docs-phishing-attack-currently-sweeping-internet); [Threatpost](https://threatpost.com/1-million-gmail-users-impacted-by-google-docs-phishing-attack/125436)
- Google said fewer than 0.1% of Gmail users were affected, which is roughly 1 million people. It stopped the campaign "within approximately one hour" by disabling the offending accounts, and said contact information was accessed but no other data. — [GovInfoSecurity](https://www.govinfosecurity.com/attackers-unleash-oauth-worm-via-google-docs-app-a-9888); [Tom's Hardware](https://www.tomshardware.com/news/google-responds-mass-phishing-attack,34341.html)
- Root weakness: any developer could give a new OAuth client any display name, including "Google Docs." Proofpoint later found a bypass of Google's first mitigations. — [Proofpoint](https://www.proofpoint.com/de/node/24991)
- Lesson for Owl: an app's display name and branding on the consent screen are attacker-controlled input. Do not show unverified app names prominently.

**Microsoft illicit consent grant / "verified publisher" abuse (Dec 2022, disclosed 2023-01-31) [V]**
- Attackers created malicious multi-tenant OAuth apps and obtained Microsoft's "verified publisher" badge through fraudulent Microsoft Cloud Partner Program accounts that impersonated real companies. Targets were UK and Ireland users. A user who consented gave the attacker far-reaching delegated mail and file permissions. Proofpoint found the campaign on 2022-12-06, Microsoft detected it on 2022-12-15, and it ended on 2022-12-27. — [Proofpoint blog](https://www.proofpoint.com/us/blog/cloud-security/dangerous-consequences-threat-actors-abusing-microsofts-verified-publisher); [Redmond Magazine](https://redmondmag.com/articles/2023/01/31/consent-phishing-attack-passed-microsoft-verified-publisher-checks.aspx)
- Microsoft disabled the apps and the fraudulent partner accounts and tightened partner vetting. A 2021 campaign had instead used compromised legitimate verified-publisher accounts. — [BleepingComputer](https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/security/microsoft-disables-verified-partner-accounts-used-for-oauth-phishing/amp/); [The Hacker News](https://thehackernews.com/2023/02/hackers-abused-microsofts-verified.html)
- Lesson: a verification badge raises the attacker's cost but is not a trust boundary. Proofpoint advised against trusting apps on verified status alone.

**Microsoft platform change: user consent blocked by default (rollout mid-July to Aug 2025) [S]**
- Message Center MC1097272 (published 2025-06-17, updated 2025-07-18): "Microsoft managed App Consent Policies" are turned on by default in all M365 tenants, so users can no longer consent to third-party apps reaching their files and sites. Users ask an admin through the admin consent workflow instead. Tenants that already have custom or stricter settings are not affected. — [MC1097272 mirror (mc.merill.net)](https://mc.merill.net/message/MC1097272/v/2025-06-18T06-14-42-383Z)
- This is a large platform reversal: after years of consent phishing, Microsoft moved high-risk scopes from user consent to admin consent by default.

**ShinyHunters / UNC6040 vishing to authorize a malicious connected app (reported 2025-06-04) [V]**
- GTIG: callers posed as internal IT support and walked employees through Salesforce's connected-app setup page. The employee approved a modified Data Loader app with a different name, for example "My Ticket Portal," or entered a connection code that linked the attacker's Data Loader to the org. Bulk exfiltration started immediately, often over Mullvad VPN IPs. About 20 organizations were hit. Extortion sometimes came months later, from a separate cluster GTIG tracks as UNC6240. — [GTIG: voice phishing data extortion](https://cloud.google.com/blog/topics/threat-intelligence/voice-phishing-data-extortion); [The Register, 2025-06-04](https://www.theregister.com/2025/06/04/fake_it_support_calls_hit/)
- The attackers also asked for credentials and MFA codes directly. GTIG published proactive hardening guidance: restrict connected-app authorization to admins, add IP restrictions, and monitor API and Data Loader use. — [GTIG: UNC6040 hardening](https://cloud.google.com/blog/topics/threat-intelligence/unc6040-proactive-hardening-recommendations); [CSO Online](https://www.csoonline.com/article/4001744/hackers-use-vishing-to-breach-salesforce-customers-and-swipe-data.html)
- Salesforce's platform response, from early September 2025: uninstalled connected apps are blocked for new users. Uninstalled apps using the OAuth 2.0 **device flow** are blocked even for users who already authorized them. Bypass needs the new "Approve Uninstalled Connected Apps" permission (Summer '25) or "Use Any API Client." — [Salesforce Admins blog](https://admin.salesforce.com/blog/2025/get-ready-for-changes-to-connected-app-usage-restrictions); [Salesforce Ben](https://www.salesforceben.com/salesforce-hardens-connected-apps-security-amid-social-engineering-attacks/)
- Lesson for Owl: the device-code / "enter this code" flow is the flow most open to phishing, because the victim approves on their own trusted device while the attacker holds the session. Salesforce singled it out for blocking.

**Salesloft Drift OAuth token theft hitting Salesforce tenants (UNC6395, 2025-08-08 to 08-18, GTIG advisory 2025-08-26) [V]**
- The attacker used OAuth access and refresh tokens stolen from the Salesloft Drift integration to bulk-export data from many corporate Salesforce instances with SOQL. The goal was to harvest secrets from records (AWS AKIA keys, passwords, Snowflake tokens). The attacker deleted query jobs to cover its tracks, but the event logs, including UniqueQuery, survived. — [GTIG advisory](https://cloud.google.com/blog/topics/threat-intelligence/data-theft-salesforce-instances-via-salesloft-drift?hl=en)
- Scope grew beyond Salesforce: "Drift Email" tokens for Google Workspace were also compromised. Google revoked those tokens and disabled the Workspace–Drift integration. GTIG told customers to treat every token stored in or connected to Drift as compromised. — [GTIG advisory](https://cloud.google.com/blog/topics/threat-intelligence/data-theft-salesforce-instances-via-salesloft-drift?hl=en); [The Hacker News](https://thehackernews.com/2025/08/google-warns-salesloft-oauth-breach.html)
- Platform response: Salesloft and Salesforce revoked all active access and refresh tokens for the Drift app, and Salesforce removed Drift from AppExchange. GTIG stressed this was not a Salesforce platform vulnerability. — [GTIG advisory](https://cloud.google.com/blog/topics/threat-intelligence/data-theft-salesforce-instances-via-salesloft-drift?hl=en); [Astrix](https://astrix.security/learn/blog/salesforce-oauth-token-breach-gtig-advisory/)
- Gap: this session did not verify how the attacker first got into Salesloft's environment. Secondary reports mention a GitHub account compromise in March–June 2025, which is **[S], not verified here**.

**Gainsight connected apps (Salesforce detection and revocation 2025-11-19/20) [S]**
- Salesforce detected unusual activity through Gainsight-published connected apps, revoked all access and refresh tokens tied to them, and temporarily pulled them from AppExchange. It stated there was no platform vulnerability. GTIG linked the activity to ShinyHunters-associated actors compromising OAuth tokens. Sources disagree on whether the date was Nov 19 or Nov 20. — [BleepingComputer](https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/security/salesforce-investigates-customer-data-theft-via-gainsight-breach/amp/); [Cybersecurity Dive](https://cybersecuritydive.com/news/salesforce-investigating-customer-connected-Gainsight/806093/)
- The number of affected tenants and the root cause at Gainsight were not confirmed from primary sources in this session.

**Context.ai → Vercel (Context.ai AWS intrusion ~Feb–Mar 2026; Vercel disclosure 2026-04-19/20) [V for core facts, S for initial vector]**
- A Vercel employee had signed up for Context.ai's consumer "AI Office Suite" with a corporate Google Workspace account and granted broad ("Allow All") OAuth scopes. Context.ai's environment was breached, and consumer users' OAuth tokens were stolen. One of those tokens was used to take over the employee's Google account and reach Vercel internal systems. A limited subset of customers had non-sensitive environment variables exposed. — [TechCrunch, 2026-04-20](https://techcrunch.com/2026/04/20/app-host-vercel-confirms-security-incident-says-customer-data-was-stolen-via-breach-at-context-ai/); [Trend Micro](https://www.trendmicro.com/en_us/research/26/d/vercel-breach-oauth-supply-chain.html); [SpecterOps](https://specterops.io/blog/2026/04/21/the-vercel-breach-explains-why-identity-attack-path-management-cant-wait/)
- Context.ai said it had stopped unauthorized access to its AWS environment in March 2026 and first notified only one customer. After Vercel's disclosure, it said the incident was likely broader. Vercel published the malicious OAuth **client ID as an IOC** so every Workspace admin could search their grants for it. — [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/04/20/app-host-vercel-confirms-security-incident-says-customer-data-was-stolen-via-breach-at-context-ai/); [Reco](https://www.reco.ai/blog/vercel-context-ai-breach)
- Conflict: Trend Micro says the initial vector was a Lumma Stealer infection of a Context.ai employee; OX Security says the employee downloaded game exploits. OX also claims the data was offered for $2M on BreachForums, and reports that "hundreds of organizations" were exposed. Neither claim was verified here. — [Trend Micro](https://www.trendmicro.com/en_us/research/26/d/vercel-breach-oauth-supply-chain.html); [OX Security](https://www.ox.security/blog/vercel-context-ai-supply-chain-attack-breachforums/)
- Lesson for Owl, and the incident most relevant to its AI-client/MCP model: an **AI tool that a consumer connects to a sensitive account with broad scopes** becomes a token warehouse. When that AI vendor is breached, every downstream grant is exposed.

### Inferences
- Since 2025 the main pattern is **integrator token-warehouse compromise**: one breach exposes N tenants. It has overtaken phishing of individual users. Owl's AI-client/MCP connectors are exactly this kind of hub. Each third-party app or AI client that holds Owl refresh tokens for many users is a single point of failure for all of them.
- In every recent case the platform could revoke all tokens for one client ID within hours to days. That is the most important kill-switch capability for Owl to build first.
- The vishing and device-code cases mean the consent flow itself must resist social engineering. Show the exact client, publisher and requested scopes. Avoid code-entry flows for high-sensitivity scopes, or use them only with strong warnings and cooldowns.

### Gaps
- Exact affected-tenant counts for Drift, Gainsight and Context.ai were not available from primary sources.
- The initial access vector for Salesloft and for Context.ai is contested or unverified.
- No public metric was found showing the effect of Microsoft's 2025 default-consent change on consent-phishing rates.

## 2. Over-collection and resale by "legitimate" apps

### Takeaway
The most damaging leaks of personal data in OAuth/SDK ecosystems came from apps with permission that collected more than they needed and then resold or repurposed it: Cambridge Analytica, Gmail apps whose staff read mail, location SDKs feeding brokers, health apps feeding ad pixels. Malicious intruders were not the main source. Enforcement converged on three tools: **sensitive-location and sensitive-data use bans, affirmative express consent for any sharing, and mandatory deletion of derived data and models**. Aggregated "anonymized" outputs such as the Strava heatmap can still reveal sensitive places.

### Cited Findings

**Facebook / Cambridge Analytica (exposed 2018-03; FTC order 2019-07) [T]**
- A personality-quiz app with about 270k installers used Facebook's friends-data permissions to collect data on up to about 87 million people. Facebook cut Graph API access in April 2018. — [Facebook newsroom, 2018-04-04](https://about.fb.com/news/2018/04/restricting-data-access/) [T]
- The FTC imposed a $5 billion penalty plus new privacy restrictions, including oversight of third-party developers. — [FTC press release, 2019-07-24](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2019/07/ftc-imposes-5-billion-penalty-sweeping-new-privacy-restrictions-facebook) [T]
- Facebook's App Developer Investigation, started March 2018, reviewed "millions" of apps. On 2019-09-20 it announced it had suspended "tens of thousands" of apps from about 400 developers, many because the developer did not respond to a request for information. Unsealed Massachusetts court documents put the figure at 69,000 suspended apps. — [Axios, 2019-09-20](https://www.axios.com/2019/09/20/facebook-suspend-thousands-apps-developer-probe); [Inquirer](https://www.inquirer.com/business/facebook-supsends-thousands-apps-cambridge-analytica-20190920.html)
- Lesson: "data about friends" means data about bystanders. It is the closest analog to Owl transcripts, which contain bystander speech. One user's consent cannot license a third party to take other people's data.

**Gmail third-party apps reading mail (WSJ, 2018-07-02) [V]**
- Return Path employees read about 8,000 full customer emails to train software. Edison Software employees reviewed emails of hundreds of users to build a feature. Both said their user agreements allowed it. — [Engadget](https://www.engadget.com/2018-07-03-third-party-app-developers-reading-gmail.html); [MacRumors](https://www.macrumors.com/2018/07/02/third-party-email-apps-reading-user-emails/)
- Google's response was Project Strobe (announced 2018-10-08): restricted Gmail scopes, mandatory app review from 2019-01-15, and a third-party security assessment for any app that sends restricted-scope data to its own servers. Apps that failed to comply lost Gmail API access. The assessment later became the tiered **CASA** framework and is required **annually**. Apps using narrow scopes such as `drive.file` are exempt, which is an incentive to request less. — [Google blog: Project Strobe](https://blog.google/technology/safety-security/project-strobe/); [Google Developers blog](https://developers.googleblog.com/2018/10/elevating-user-trust-in-our-api.html); [Google Cloud: Security Assessment](https://support.google.com/cloud/answer/13465431); [9to5Google: Drive](https://9to5google.com/2019/05/30/google-drive-project-strobe/)
- Lesson: human access to user content at the integrator (staff reading mail for ML training) was technically within scope. Only policy plus audits addressed it. Owl transcripts are equally attractive as training data.

**Location data brokers and SDK resale**
- **X-Mode/Outlogic (FTC, 2024-01)**: the FTC's first order banning a data broker from selling sensitive location data. — [FTC press release](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2024/01/ftc-order-prohibits-data-broker-x-mode-social-outlogic-selling-sensitive-location-data) [T]
- **Gravy Analytics / Venntel and Mobilewalla (proposed 2024-12-03, finalized 2025-01-14, vote 5-0 for Gravy) [V]**: Gravy and Venntel are barred from selling, disclosing or using sensitive location data, with narrow national-security and law-enforcement exceptions, and must run a "sensitive location data program." Mobilewalla must keep a list of sensitive locations and block disclosure of visits to them. — [FTC press release, 2025-01](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/01/ftc-finalizes-order-prohibiting-gravy-analytics-venntel-selling-sensitive-location-data); [The Record](https://therecord.media/ftc-location-data-brokers-gravy-venntel-mobilewalla); [Hunton](https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-cybersecurity-law-blog/ftc-finalizes-orders-against-data-brokers-over-sensitive-location-data)
- **Gravy Analytics breach (2025-01)**: hackers claimed to have stolen location data, much of it derived from ad bidstream. Reported by 404 Media and others. **[T, not verified in this session]**
- **Kochava [V]**: the FTC sued in 2022. In 2025-02 an Idaho federal judge denied Kochava's motion to dismiss the amended complaint. A private class action settled in 2025-11 with injunctive relief, including a "privacy block" filtering sensitive-location data. The FTC announced a proposed settlement on **2026-05-04**: Kochava and its subsidiary Collective Data Solutions are banned from selling precise location data without explicit consent, with no admission of the allegations. The FTC vote was 2-0, and court entry was pending at the time of reporting. — [Proskauer, 2026-06-03](https://newmedialaw.proskauer.com/2026/06/03/ftc-remains-on-the-location-data-beat-agency-settles-long-running-kochava-enforcement-even-as-state-regulation-has-already-changed-the-landscape/); [White & Case](https://www.whitecase.com/insight-alert/ftc-settles-data-broker-kochava-over-sale-sensitive-location-data-key-takeaways); [HIPAA Journal](https://www.hipaajournal.com/ftcs-amended-complaint-against-kochava-survives-motion-to-dismiss/)
- **Fog Data Science (reported 2022-08/09)**: sold police access to app-derived geolocation data ("billions" of points from "over 250 million" devices) **without a warrant**. AP found nearly two dozen agencies under contract. — [EFF](https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2022/08/fog-data-science-puts-our-fourth-amendment-rights-sale); [The Conversation](https://theconversation.com/what-is-fog-reveal-a-legal-scholar-explains-the-app-some-police-forces-are-using-to-track-people-without-a-warrant-189944)

**Health and fitness data sharing**
- **Flo Health (FTC settlement 2021-01, no admission)**: the FTC alleged the period tracker shared health data with Facebook and Google despite promising privacy. — [Crowell](https://www.crowell.com/en/insights/client-alerts/ftc-enforcement-against-sharing-consumer-health-information-continues) [S]
- **Frasco v. Flo Health jury verdict (2025-08-01, N.D. Cal.) [V]**: the jury found **Meta** liable under the California Invasion of Privacy Act (CIPA) for eavesdropping on Flo in-app events through its SDK without all-party consent. Flo, Google and Flurry settled before the verdict. Post-trial motions were reportedly denied, and damages were undetermined in the sources (statutory exposure up to $5,000 per violation). — [Lawdragon, 2025-08-25](https://www.lawdragon.com/news-features/2025-08-25-big-tech-on-trial-jury-finds-meta-liable-for-misusing-women-health-data); [National Law Review](https://natlawreview.com/article/federal-jury-finds-against-meta-collecting-data-flo-health); [court order, govinfo](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCOURTS-cand-3_21-cv-00757/pdf/USCOURTS-cand-3_21-cv-00757-9.pdf)
- **GoodRx (2023-02)**: the first enforcement under the Health Breach Notification Rule. $1.5M civil penalty and a ban on sharing health data for advertising. One outlet says $1.7M, which conflicts. — [HIPAA Journal](https://www.hipaajournal.com/court-approves-ftc-settlement-goodrx/); [Healthcare Dive](https://www.healthcaredive.com/news/ftc-betterhelp-settlement-health-data-sharing-fine/644021/)
- **BetterHelp (2023-03)**: $7.8M for sharing mental-health data with Facebook, Snapchat, Criteo and Pinterest, brought as a deception case rather than under the Health Breach Notification Rule. — [EPIC](https://epic.org/?p=32185); [MobiHealthNews](https://www.mobihealthnews.com/news/ftc-bar-betterhelp-sharing-health-data-ads)

**Strava global heatmap (public 2017-11, exposed 2018-01-27/28) [V]**
- Analyst Nathan Ruser showed that the aggregated "anonymized" heatmap of over 1 billion activities outlined military bases and patrol routes in Syria and Afghanistan. Strava said it already excluded private activities and privacy zones. It later changed the map and updated it monthly. The US DoD reviewed its policies. — [Help Net Security, 2018-01-29](https://www.helpnetsecurity.com/2018/01/29/strava-user-heatmap-reveals-patterns-of-life-in-western-military-bases/); [NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/news/amp/ncna841871); [Cycling Weekly](https://cyclingweekly.com/news/latest-news/strava-updates-heatmap-revealed-locations-secret-military-bases-372967)

### Inferences
- Owl's GPS history plus transcripts falls into the FTC's "sensitive data" category (sensitive locations, health, religion and sexuality inferable from speech). The FTC remedy pattern (sensitive-location blocklists, affirmative express consent, no resale, deletion of derived data) gives Owl a ready-made **developer policy template**: bind third-party apps to it contractually, and enforce it technically where possible, for example by geofencing out sensitive places before API delivery.
- A ban on onward transfer and ad/SDK pixels within third-party apps matters. In Flo, GoodRx and BetterHelp the leak ran through embedded ad SDKs, not through the API itself.
- Strava shows that aggregates and derived features (heatmaps, "frequent places," speaker statistics) need their own threat model. "Anonymized" is not a safe default for location.

### Gaps
- No public metric was found for the effect of CASA or Project Strobe, such as the number of apps rejected or the change in incidents. Google publishes the policy, not the outcome numbers.
- The X-Mode and Cambridge Analytica URLs were not re-fetched this session ([T]).
- Kochava court entry status as of November 2026 was not confirmed.

## 3. Token theft and leakage at integrators; what platforms changed afterwards

### Takeaway
Long-lived OAuth refresh tokens and API secrets held by integrators, and by support systems through HAR files, are the recurring weak point. In 2022 (Heroku/Travis), 2023 (Okta), 2024 (Sisense) and 2025–26 (Drift, Gainsight, Context.ai) the fix followed the same playbook: platform-wide revocation per client, customer notification, IOC publication, and urging customers to rotate everything. Durable platform changes came afterwards: blocking unapproved apps, restricting device flow, default admin consent, and sanitizing HAR files.

### Cited Findings
- **GitHub / Heroku / Travis CI (detected 2022-04-12, disclosed 2022-04-15) [V]**: an attacker used OAuth user tokens stolen from Heroku and Travis CI (not from GitHub) to download private repositories of dozens of organizations, including npm's. That yielded an AWS key into npm infrastructure. Heroku revoked all existing GitHub tokens by 2022-04-17 and paused its GitHub integration. GitHub sent final victim notifications by 2022-04-27. — [GitHub blog](https://github.blog/news-insights/company-news/security-alert-stolen-oauth-user-tokens/); [Security Affairs](https://securityaffairs.com/posts/github-warns-stolen-oauth-tokens-access-data)
  - Detection signal: GitHub first spotted the intrusion through unusual use of an AWS key obtained this way, then traced it to anomalous repository listing and cloning by integrator tokens. **[T for the AWS-key detection detail; this session confirmed only that GitHub's own investigation found it.]**
- **Okta support system (2023-09-28 to 10-17; disclosed 2023-10-20) [V/S]**: stolen credentials gave access to the support case system. HAR files uploaded by 134 customers (<1%) contained **live session tokens**, which were used to hijack admin sessions. BeyondTrust confirmed this happened to it. Secondary reports also name 1Password and Cloudflare. Okta revoked the embedded session tokens and recommended sanitizing HAR files. A 2023-11-29 update said a report of support-system user names and emails had been downloaded; how far that exposure reached is reported inconsistently. — [BleepingComputer](https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/security/okta-breach-134-customers-exposed-in-october-support-system-hack/); [BeyondTrust](https://beyondtrust.com/blog/entry/okta-support-unit-breach); [Security Affairs update](https://securityaffairs.com/154965/hacking/okta-update-october-2023-support-system-breach.html)
  - Notable: customers (BeyondTrust, Cloudflare), not Okta, detected the session hijacks first, by spotting admin activity from unexpected IPs.
- **Sisense (CISA alert 2024-04-11) [V]**: CISA told Sisense customers to reset all credentials and secrets shared with or used by the BI service, which held many customers' database and API credentials. Sisense told customers to look for activity from 2024-04-05 onward. — [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2024/04/11/cisa-government-sisense-reset-credentials-cyberattack); [Infosecurity Magazine](https://www.infosecurity-magazine.com/news/cisa-urges-reset-sisense-breach/)
- **Salesloft Drift, Gainsight, Context.ai**: see section 1. All are refresh-token warehouse thefts. In Drift, the attacker went after *secrets stored inside the victim's data* (AWS keys in Salesforce records), so the data itself became a source of further compromise. — [GTIG](https://cloud.google.com/blog/topics/threat-intelligence/data-theft-salesforce-instances-via-salesloft-drift?hl=en)

What platforms changed afterwards:
- Salesforce (Sept 2025): blocked uninstalled connected apps for new users, blocked device flow for uninstalled apps entirely, and added explicit permissions to bypass. — [Salesforce Admins](https://admin.salesforce.com/blog/2025/get-ready-for-changes-to-connected-app-usage-restrictions)
- Microsoft (Jul–Aug 2025): user consent for file and site scopes is now off by default, with an admin consent workflow. — [MC1097272](https://mc.merill.net/message/MC1097272/v/2025-06-18T06-14-42-383Z)
- Google (2025-08): revoked Drift Email tokens and disabled the integration for all Workspace customers. This is a platform-side kill switch used without waiting for each customer. — [GTIG](https://cloud.google.com/blog/topics/threat-intelligence/data-theft-salesforce-instances-via-salesloft-drift?hl=en)

### Inferences
- For Owl the controls are concrete:
  1. Short-lived access tokens plus **sender-constrained or rotating refresh tokens** (DPoP/mTLS binding or refresh-token rotation with reuse detection), so a token stolen in bulk from an integrator's database is useless or detectable.
  2. Per-client **mass revocation** with one action, plus suspension from the directory.
  3. Per-client **IOC publication**, and a user-facing notice saying "App X was compromised; your grant was revoked."
  4. Never accept HAR files or debug dumps containing tokens in support channels; scrub them automatically.
  5. Tell integrators not to store Owl data alongside secrets.
- The UniqueQuery-style **query audit logs** survived even when the attacker deleted jobs. Owl should keep immutable, per-client query and export logs that the third party cannot delete.

### Gaps
- No public, quantified time-to-detect metrics were found for these incidents beyond the date ranges above.
- This session did not directly re-verify GitHub's specific detection signal or Heroku's root cause.

## 4. Platform controls that worked (and measurable outcomes)

### Takeaway
The controls with the best documented track record are structural, not detective: restricted sensitive scopes plus mandatory review and annual third-party assessment (Google Strobe/CASA), admin-only or block-by-default consent (Microsoft 2025, Salesforce 2025), and the ability to revoke all tokens per app and delist the app within hours (Google 2017, Salesforce/Salesloft 2025, Salesforce/Gainsight 2025). Detection of bulk exports came mostly from logs reviewed after the fact, or from downstream canaries such as misuse of AWS keys. Real-time platform anomaly detection is mentioned, but its outcomes are rarely quantified in public.

### Cited Findings
- **App review + restricted scopes + annual assessment**: Google restricted Gmail and Drive scopes, required verification from 2019-01-15, required a third-party assessment for server-side storage (now CASA, annual and tiered by user count and scope), suspended noncompliant apps, and exempted narrow scopes. — [Google blog](https://blog.google/technology/safety-security/project-strobe/); [Google support](https://support.google.com/cloud/answer/13465431)
- **Developer audits with suspension on non-response**: Facebook suspended "tens of thousands" of apps from about 400 developers, many simply for failing to answer requests for information. This is a cheap, scalable lever. — [Axios](https://www.axios.com/2019/09/20/facebook-suspend-thousands-apps-developer-probe)
- **Default-deny consent**: Microsoft managed app consent policies (2025). Salesforce blocks uninstalled apps and the device flow (2025). — [MC1097272](https://mc.merill.net/message/MC1097272/v/2025-06-18T06-14-42-383Z); [Salesforce Admins](https://admin.salesforce.com/blog/2025/get-ready-for-changes-to-connected-app-usage-restrictions)
- **Kill switch / mass revocation and delisting**: Google stopped the Docs worm in about 1 hour (2017). Salesloft and Salesforce revoked all Drift tokens and delisted Drift (2025-08). Salesforce did the same for Gainsight apps (2025-11). Google revoked Drift Email tokens. — [Tom's Hardware](https://www.tomshardware.com/news/google-responds-mass-phishing-attack,34341.html); [GTIG](https://cloud.google.com/blog/topics/threat-intelligence/data-theft-salesforce-instances-via-salesloft-drift?hl=en); [BleepingComputer](https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/security/salesforce-investigates-customer-data-theft-via-gainsight-breach/amp/)
- **IP allowlisting / network restriction for integrations**: GTIG recommended IP-based login restrictions and limiting connected-app approval to admins after UNC6040. — [GTIG hardening](https://cloud.google.com/blog/topics/threat-intelligence/unc6040-proactive-hardening-recommendations)
- **Bulk-export forensics**: GTIG pointed victims to Salesforce event logs (UniqueQuery) to scope SOQL exfiltration. The logs survived the attacker deleting query jobs. — [GTIG](https://cloud.google.com/blog/topics/threat-intelligence/data-theft-salesforce-instances-via-salesloft-drift?hl=en)
- **Publishing the client ID as an IOC** let every Workspace tenant self-check. — [TechCrunch / Vercel](https://techcrunch.com/2026/04/20/app-host-vercel-confirms-security-incident-says-customer-data-was-stolen-via-breach-at-context-ai/)
- **Verification badges are not enough on their own**: the 2022 verified-publisher abuse. — [Proofpoint](https://www.proofpoint.com/us/blog/cloud-security/dangerous-consequences-threat-actors-abusing-microsofts-verified-publisher)
- **Aggregation and privacy-zone defaults**: Strava's privacy zones and private-activity exclusion did not stop base exposure. It added monthly updates and other changes. — [Cycling Weekly](https://cyclingweekly.com/news/latest-news/strava-updates-heatmap-revealed-locations-secret-military-bases-372967)

### Inferences (controls mapped to Owl)
- **Tier the scopes.** For example: `summary:read` (low), `transcript:read` (restricted), `location:history` (restricted), `export:bulk` (separately restricted, possibly never granted to third parties). Restricted tiers need review plus an annual security assessment, CASA-style, before going beyond a small user cap. Per Google's practice, a narrow scope can skip the assessment, which gives developers an incentive to ask for less.
- **No bulk scope for AI/MCP clients by default.** Give them query-scoped, time-windowed, rate-limited access, and alert on export-shaped access patterns: high-cardinality reads across a user's full history, or many users from one client in a short window (the Drift/UNC6040 pattern).
- **A user-facing grant notice** (email/push on every new grant, "App X can now read your transcripts") plus **periodic re-consent** for restricted scopes, for example every 90 days. Grants then expire unless renewed. This also limits a stalker's long-lived access (see section 5).
- **Keep per-client, immutable audit logs** that are visible to the user: which app read which day's transcript, when, and from which IP.
- **A bug bounty that covers third-party data misuse**: Facebook's Data Abuse Bounty (launched 2018-04) is a precedent. **[T, not verified in this session]**

### Gaps
- No public, quantified outcome data was found for anomaly detection on API access (for example, "X% of abusive apps caught by rate-limit anomalies"). Platforms describe it qualitatively.
- No measured effect was found for periodic re-consent or new-grant notifications on abuse rates. Their basis is design reasoning plus the incident patterns, not published metrics.
- Facebook's Data Abuse Bounty details (dates, payouts, findings) were not verified this session.

## 5. Special risk of always-on audio and location data in third-party hands

### Takeaway
Owl's data is unusually dangerous for three reasons. (1) It contains **bystanders** who never consented, the Cambridge Analytica "friends data" problem in audio form. (2) Once copied to a third party it is reachable by **law enforcement without the warrant protection** that applies to the platform (the Fog Data Science / data-broker pattern). (3) It is ideal for **intimate partner surveillance**: an abuser with brief physical access to the victim's phone or account can link the victim's Owl to an app the abuser controls. Research shows dual-use apps are as powerful as spyware and that anti-virus tools fail to flag them. The best precedents are Apple's Safety Check (review and revoke all access) and the Apple–Google unwanted-tracker alerts (notify the tracked person, not only the owner).

### Cited Findings
- **Bystanders**: Facebook's friends-permission let one consenting user expose up to about 87M people, which led to the API cutback and the FTC order. — [Facebook newsroom](https://about.fb.com/news/2018/04/restricting-data-access/) [T]; [FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2019/07/ftc-imposes-5-billion-penalty-sweeping-new-privacy-restrictions-facebook) [T]
- **Consent law for recorded communications**: the 2025 Flo/Meta verdict turned on CIPA's **all-party consent** requirement for intercepting communications. An SDK that recorded in-app events was enough. Third parties that receive audio-derived transcripts of conversations may face similar all-party-consent exposure in two-party-consent states. — [National Law Review](https://natlawreview.com/article/federal-jury-finds-against-meta-collecting-data-flo-health)
- **Law-enforcement access via third parties**: Fog Data Science sold police app-derived location data without a warrant, sidestepping *Carpenter* (2018), which requires a warrant for carrier location history. AP found nearly two dozen agencies with contracts. — [EFF](https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2022/08/fog-data-science-puts-our-fourth-amendment-rights-sale); [The Conversation](https://theconversation.com/what-is-fog-reveal-a-legal-scholar-explains-the-app-some-police-forces-are-using-to-track-people-without-a-warrant-189944). The FTC's Gravy/Venntel order explicitly carves out national-security and law-enforcement uses. — [FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/01/ftc-finalizes-order-prohibiting-gravy-analytics-venntel-selling-sensitive-location-data)
- **Intimate partner surveillance via dual-use apps**: Chatterjee et al. (IEEE S&P 2018) measured 61 on-store and 9 off-store apps usable for intimate partner surveillance. Dual-use apps (child safety, anti-theft) "are often as powerful as overt spyware." Anti-virus flagged at most 13% of on-store dual-use apps. 8 of 11 app support teams contacted gave answers that implicitly condoned partner surveillance. — [Chatterjee et al., IEEE S&P 2018 (PDF)](https://www.cs.cornell.edu/~rahul/papers/IPV_Spyware.pdf); [Cornell Chronicle](https://news.cornell.edu/node/302176)
- **Scale of stalkerware**: Kaspersky counted 31,031 unique mobile users affected by stalkerware in 2023, up from 29,312 in 2022. These are only Kaspersky-protected users, so this is a floor. — [Kaspersky, State of Stalkerware 2023 (PDF)](https://go.kaspersky.com/rs/802-IJN-240/images/The-State-of-Stalkerware-in-2023-final-5-03-24.pdf?version=0)
- **Apple Safety Check (iOS 16, announced 2022-06-06)**: lets someone at risk from a partner review and instantly revoke access granted to people **and apps**: location sharing, app permissions, devices on the Apple ID, and trusted numbers. It includes an "Emergency Reset" and was built with the National Network to End Domestic Violence (NNEDV). — [MacRumors](https://www.macrumors.com/2022/06/06/ios-16-safety-check-abusive-relationships/); [Apple Personal Safety guide](https://support.apple.com/en-nz/guide/personal-safety/ips5f85e089f/web)
- **Apple–Google "Detecting Unwanted Location Trackers"** (spec submitted to the IETF 2023-05; shipped in iOS 17.5 and Android 6.0+ in 2024-05): alerts the *person being tracked* when a tracker separated from its owner travels with them, and explains how to find and disable it. Chipolo, eufy, Jio, Motorola and Pebblebee committed to support it. — [Apple Newsroom, 2023-05](https://www.apple.com/newsroom/2023/05/apple-google-partner-on-an-industry-specification-to-address-unwanted-tracking/); [Apple Newsroom, 2024-05](https://apple.com/newsroom/2024/05/apple-and-google-deliver-support-for-unwanted-tracking-alerts-in-ios-and-android)
- The status of the IETF DULT working group (whether an RFC has been published) was **not verified**.

### Inferences (anti-abuser account-linking design for Owl)
- **Threat:** an abuser with brief access to the victim's unlocked phone or Owl account approves an OAuth grant, or completes a device-code pairing, to an app the abuser controls. They then read the victim's transcripts and location remotely for months. The victim receives no signal. This is exactly the dual-use pattern Chatterjee describes, and the device-flow phishing pattern Salesforce had to block.
- **Mitigations drawn from these precedents:**
  1. **Step-up authentication at grant time** for restricted scopes: passkey/biometric on the account owner's device, plus a delay of 24–72h before restricted scopes go live, during which the user is notified repeatedly.
  2. **Out-of-band notice on every new grant** through every channel the user has (email, push, and an LED or haptic signal on the pendant itself). This is the "alert the tracked person" principle from DULT applied to API grants.
  3. **Recurring reminders.** An Owl version of iOS's location-sharing reminder: "App X has had access to your transcripts for 30 days."
  4. **A Safety Check equivalent**: one tap that revokes every third-party grant and active session and rotates credentials, reachable from the device and from a web page that needs no login state on the possibly compromised phone.
  5. **Block or flag apps** that request continuous location or transcript streaming to a *different* Owl account holder. Treat "monitor another person" use cases as prohibited in developer policy. Note that Chatterjee found dual-use marketing ("track your partner") is common.
  6. **No device-code flow** for restricted scopes.
  7. Keep a **directory-level abuse report channel** and partner with groups such as the Coalition Against Stalkerware / NNEDV, following Apple's model.
- **Bystander and law-enforcement exposure:** minimize what leaves Owl. Restrict third-party scopes to the user's own speech or summaries by default, redact bystander speech, and require that third parties not retain raw data. Restricted-scope terms should require the third party to notify users of legal process and to publish a transparency report, because Fog-style purchases show downstream holders become the soft target for warrantless access.

### Gaps
- No public research was found specifically on intimate partner abuse through **OAuth grants to consumer lifelogging or wearable APIs**. The threat model above is extrapolated from dual-use app research, Apple Safety Check and the tracker specs.
- The Coalition Against Stalkerware's current membership, detection criteria and 2024–2026 figures were not retrieved this session. No Kaspersky 2024/2025 stalkerware figures were found.
- No figures were found on subpoenas or law-enforcement demands made to third-party integrators of wearable or voice platforms. Amazon Ring ending its "Request for Assistance" police tool in 2024 is a possible precedent, but this session did not verify it **[unverified]**.
